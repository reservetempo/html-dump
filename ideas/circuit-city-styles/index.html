Style reference board · researched November 2026

Circuit CityWhat a city builder that teaches computer architecture could look like, from the first adder to a working ALU

Bottom line

  • Nobody has shipped this. Circuit puzzle games (Turing Complete, Nandgame, Chip Builder) go from gates to a CPU with no city. Colony games (Oxygen Not Included, Timberborn) have gate buildings but teach automation, not architecture.16,20,30,37
  • Small teams that ship city builders pick minimal or procedural looks: Mini Metro was made by two people, Islanders by three, and Townscaper by one.1,3,5 The cheapest styles below are transit-map, blueprint, voxel and neon. Low-poly 3D gives the most "city" for the effort (a judgement call, not a measurement).
  • Traffic works as signals with one rule: the road's state is the bit, and every gate is a depot that sends out its own trucks, powered by the power plant. Travel time then becomes propagation delay you can watch. Every game that added logic also shows it on a separate on/off overlay, so plan one.16,32,39
  • Biggest risk: existing games already teach this well. The city has to earn its place by making timing and hierarchy easier to see than a schematic does.

Confidence: medium-high on styles and prior art; medium on the traffic metaphor (the physics is sound, but nobody has tested it as an interface).

Sheet B

The closest existing games

The research found no shipped city builder that teaches CPU design. The nearest games come from two directions. Circuit puzzle games take you from gates to a CPU but have no city. Colony and factory games have logic-gate buildings but use them to automate things, not to teach how a computer works.16,17,20

Near misses and odd relatives

  • CPU City (Kyle Jussab, 2024): a Unity student project where a city hides the von Neumann architecture. It's a story-driven quest game rather than a builder, and reports 92% positive playtest feedback.31
  • A 4-bit adder in Cities: Skylines (Dániel Bali, 2019). The bits were power lines being powered or unpowered, not traffic.32
  • Gates from trains in OpenTTD (2005): gates and a ripple-carry adder built from rail signals.19
  • Browser-based gate-to-CPU courses like Nandgame and Digital Logic Sim use a minimal schematic look.22,29 Rocky's Boots and Robot Odyssey did logic games in the 1980s; Robot Odyssey was later called the hardest computer game ever made.34
  • "Silicon City" is an unrelated traffic sim that only shares the name.33 A US patent maps school curriculum topics onto city buildings.35

The deep search covered Steam, itch.io and game jams. Confidence that nothing closer exists is medium-high.

Sheet C

City buildings as computer parts

City builders use a fairly small set of building types. Each card pairs one type with the computer part it fits best when signals are deliveries, plus where the pairing strains. Tag colours follow SimCity-style zoning. Screenshots show games that have that kind of building, not the building itself.

These pairings are design proposals from the research, not standard mappings. Buildings standing for code does have precedent: CodeCity draws software classes as buildings and packages as districts,26 and close-up photos of chip dies look strikingly like aerial cities.27

Dropped after the deep dive: a toll booth as a gate (it implies the car passes through), and a bus route as the program counter ("bus" already means a group of data wires).

Sheet D

Traffic and deliveries as signals

The problem. A car takes one road and is gone once it arrives. A digital signal does neither. It copies itself down every branch (fan-out), and it is a level that stays on, not a packet. Delivery games that added logic all put it on a separate wire layer that shows on/off state: Factorio, shapez and Oxygen Not Included.16,39,40

The fix: the depot rule. Make the road's state the bit. A road with trucks on it is 1; an empty, dark road is 0. The trucks animate that state but are never the data. Every gate is a depot that sends out trucks from its own garage, fed by the power plant. Its input roads only tell it when to send. Real CMOS logic works the same way: the output's energy comes from the supply, and the inputs only sense. This one rule fixes both problems.

Four signal models that can be combined

A · CONVOY

A busy road is 1

A steady stream of trucks means 1; an empty road means 0. Very easy to read. In a 1983 study, people taught a "teeming crowds" picture of electricity reasoned better about resistors, while people taught a water picture did better on batteries.48

Strains: it suggests energy is spent the whole time a 1 is held. Real CMOS mostly uses power when it switches.

B · PARKING LANE

A full lane is 1

Each road is a row of parking bays. To switch to 1, the depot's trucks drive out and fill the bays; to switch to 0, they drive home. Longer roads and more branches have more bays, so they take longer to fill. That is an honest picture of wire delay, fan-out load and the energy spent switching. It matches the "parking lots as buffers" idea from a paper on city traffic as computer traffic.28

Strains: the researcher's own synthesis. The physics is sound, but nobody has tested it as a UI.

C · CLOCKED DELIVERIES

The traffic lights are the clock

Warehouses (registers) open only when the lights change. They record whether their road is full, then hold that value. If the trucks haven't arrived in time, the warehouse stores the wrong value: a timing violation you can see. The light cycle can't be shorter than the longest commute, which is the critical path.

Strains: players have to juggle two kinds of time, travel and cycles.

D · REAL PACKETS (LATE GAME)

Trucks are the data

On-chip networks really do route packets between modules,44 and asynchronous circuits pass data with request/acknowledge handshakes.42,43 Petri nets give exact rules for tokens.41

Strains: this is only true at the scale of cores and memory, well past an ALU.

Sheet E

From one depot to an industrial district

One way the adder-to-ALU path could grow as a city, using the depot rule. The names are placeholders.

  1. 01NOT gate

    Night-shift depot

    Sends trucks when its input road is quiet. The player has to connect it to the power plant, which shows that a gate's output energy comes from the supply. Deliveries from the border test it, one truth-table row per "day".

    Teaches: inversion; gates need power.

  2. 02AND · OR

    Two-key depot and either-road depot

    The two-key depot sends trucks only when both input roads are busy; the either-road depot sends them when at least one is. Optionally, build everything from a single NAND depot, as Nand2Tetris does.51

    Teaches: truth tables; one universal gate.

  3. 03XOR

    The first composite block

    (A OR B) AND NOT (A AND B). Each input now feeds two depots, so the copy roundabout first appears here. Uneven road lengths cause a brief wrong pulse: the first glitch the player can see.

    Teaches: fan-out; glitches.

  4. 04Half adder

    Sum Street and Carry Lane

    One block with two exits: Sum Street (XOR) and Carry Lane (AND). Once it passes its tests, it can be zoned into a single building.

    Teaches: binary addition; packaging a circuit as a part.

  5. 05Full adder

    Two half-adder buildings and an OR depot

    Three input roads (A, B and carry-in) now feed buildings the player made earlier.

    Teaches: reuse; carry-in.

  6. 064-bit ripple-carry adder

    Carry Street

    Four full-adder blocks along one street, passing the carry house to house. A stopwatch shows 0111 + 0001 rippling through all four. That commute is the propagation delay. The light cycle has to be longer than it, or the result warehouse stores garbage. Optional extra: an express motorway for carry-lookahead.

    Teaches: propagation delay; the critical path; why clocks have a speed limit.

  7. 07Multiplexer

    Railway-points interchange

    Points set by a selector road. First a 2:1 mux built from gates, then a 4:1 built from three 2:1s.

    Teaches: selection; building big parts from small ones.

  8. 08ALU

    The industrial district

    Four workshops run all the time, side by side: an adder; a subtractor (the same adder with B inverted and carry-in set to 1, which is two's complement); an AND row; and an OR row. City hall's 2-bit work order sets the points that pick which result leaves the district. An "empty-streets inspector" (a NOR of all outputs) raises the zero flag, and the last Carry Lane exit is the carry flag. A result warehouse at the end is the bridge to a CPU.

    Teaches: hardware computes every operation, then selects one; opcodes; flags. For comparison, the Nand2Tetris ALU has zero and negative outputs but no carry flag.51

Sheet F

The strongest case against

Going from gates to a CPU is already well taught. Turing Complete is 96% positive on Steam,20 and Nandgame, Digital Logic Sim and Chip Builder cover the same ground.22,29,61 A city adds a second picture the player has to learn and translate,60 so it must teach something a schematic can't. The best candidates are timing (you can watch a carry ripple down a street) and hierarchy (a tested block collapses into one building).

Sheet G

Eight questions to answer before designing

These cover the whole project, not just the art. Each comes with the cheapest first step toward an answer.

  1. Who is the learner, and what should they be able to do when they finish?

    Why it mattersIt sets scope, difficulty and how you'll know the game worked. Evidence that games like this teach is thin,56,57 so plan to measure it.

    Cheapest first stepFinish the sentence "After playing, a ___ can ___." Then turn it into a five-question before/after quiz for the first playtest.

  2. What can a city show that a schematic can't?

    Why it mattersTuring Complete and Nandgame already teach gates to CPU well. Without an answer, there's no reason for the city.

    Cheapest first stepList three moments that only work in a city, like watching a carry ripple down a street.

  3. Is the city where you build, or a skin over a schematic?

    Why it mattersContent built into the core mechanic beat content wrapped in a game.54

    Cheapest first stepDecide what the player's hands touch: roads and depots, or gate symbols. Mock a XOR both ways and ask 5–8 people to predict outputs for new inputs.

  4. What exactly is a signal, and how will the game admit where the metaphor breaks?

    Why it mattersEach signal model leads players to different conclusions,48 and unstated limits become a hidden curriculum.50,58

    Cheapest first stepAnimate the convoy, parking-lane and token versions for ten seconds each, then ask testers: "What happens when this road splits?"

  5. Is timing a core mechanic or hidden?

    Why it mattersTravel time as propagation delay is the delivery metaphor's biggest strength, and it's where a city beats a schematic.

    Cheapest first stepA tiny web sim of traffic-light cycle against carry delay. Is it fun to tune?

  6. What does "the city grows" mean: an economy, citizens, or just unlocks?

    Why it mattersMoney and happiness systems can fight exact, testable puzzles.

    Cheapest first stepStart with growth = unlocks only. Add sim systems only if playtesters say the city feels empty.

  7. When does a tested circuit become one building, and can players open it again?

    Why it mattersHierarchy (small parts making bigger parts) is the main idea in computer architecture. It also sets how far the game goes: just the ALU, or on to a CPU with memory.

    Cheapest first stepA paper prototype that zooms from gate to half adder to 4-bit adder.

  8. Which art style stays readable from one gate to a whole district, at a cost you can keep up?

    Why it mattersArt is the biggest cost, and the style also decides the engine (browser for classrooms, or desktop for Steam).

    Cheapest first stepGreybox a 4-bit adder in a tile editor, count the tiles, and check it fits on one screen. Then dress the same layout in two styles from Sheet A.
